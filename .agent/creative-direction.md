# Creative Direction — Michael Gikaru Portfolio

> Status: Art direction exploration. No implementation code.
> Source: `.agent/cv-analysis.md`

## DIRECTION 1: THE SYNTAX OF JUDGMENT

### 1. Concept

Michael operates at the intersection of three grammatical systems: **human language** (Swahili, English, linguistics), **code** (LSP, Tree-sitter, TypeScript, Scala), and **evaluation** (Woven scoring, AI quality assessment). This direction treats the entire site as a **living dictionary or grammar manual** — not metaphorically, but structurally.

The portfolio is organized as a lexicon of Michael's capabilities. Each project is a "headword" with "definitions" (descriptions), "etymology" (career context), and "usage notes" (technologies and outcomes). The evaluation background is reframed as **proofreading and editorial rigor** — the same skill applied to code and AI that a linguist applies to text.

### 2. Emotional Response (First 10 Seconds)

The visitor should feel: **"I have entered a precise, intellectually rigorous space."**

Not a playful hacker den, not a corporate SaaS page, but a **scholar's workshop** — warm paper textures, exacting typography, and the quiet confidence of someone who has spent years judging quality. There is an immediate sense that this person takes language and structure seriously.

### 3. Visual Vocabulary

- **Paper and ink:** Off-white backgrounds (#F7F4ED or similar warm paper), deep charcoal text (#1A1A1A), no pure black.
- **Editorial rules:** Thin horizontal rules, numbered sections, marginalia-style annotations, pull quotes.
- **Lexicon formatting:** Projects presented as dictionary entries — headword in large type, followed by numbered senses or contexts.
- **Syntax tree branches:** Actual parse-tree structures used as decorative dividers, background textures, and hover states.
- **Linguistic notation:** IPA-style characters, grammatical labels (n., v., adj.) used as category markers.
- **Swahili script integration:** Subtle use of Arabic-derived Swahili letterforms as watermarks, section markers, or hover reveals — not decorative exoticism, but structural honesty about his linguistic identity.
- **Concrete and warmth:** Raw concrete textures (from Nairobi, from construction/industrial) softened by paper and typography. Not brutalist for shock value, but as a material contrast.

### 4. Typography

- **Display / Headlines:** A sharp, contemporary serif with high contrast — e.g., *Instrument Serif*, *Playfair Display*, or *Fraunces*. Used at extreme scales (very large, very small). Conveys editorial authority.
- **Body / Narrative:** A warm, readable serif or humanist sans — e.g., *Source Serif Pro*, *Lora*, or *IBM Plex Serif*. Long-form reading comfort.
- **Code / Technical / Metadata:** A distinguished monospace — *JetBrains Mono*, *IBM Plex Mono*, or *SF Mono*. Used for technologies, dates, file paths, and evaluation metadata. Not as a "hacker" effect, but as a **register shift** signaling: "this is the technical layer."
- **Relationship:** The serif handles voice and story. The monospace handles precision and structure. The visitor's eye learns to switch registers as Michael switches between psychology, linguistics, and engineering.

### 5. Color Strategy

- **Primary background:** Warm off-white paper (#F5F1E8). Feels like a printed lexicon or field notebook.
- **Primary text:** Deep charcoal (#1C1917). Not black — softer, more human.
- **Accent 1 — Syntax highlight:** A muted indigo (#4F46E5) for LSP, Tree-sitter, code intelligence references. Derived from the blue of IDE syntax highlighting, but desaturated to fit the paper palette.
- **Accent 2 — Linguistic warmth:** Burnt sienna or terracotta (#C2410C) for Swahili, Kamusi, African language references. Derived from Kenyan earth and traditional textile dyes.
- **Accent 3 — Evaluation:** Amber/gold (#D97706) for Woven, scoring, quality, feedback. Like a highlighter or editor's mark.
- **Neutral:** Warm grays for secondary text and borders.

**Why this palette belongs to Michael:** It maps to his actual domains — paper/ink for editorial evaluation, indigo for IDE/code intelligence, terracotta for African linguistics, amber for quality assurance. The palette is not arbitrary; it is a **legend** for his professional identity.

### 6. Layout System

- **Editorial grid:** A 12-column grid with generous margins. Asymmetric two-column layouts for project entries — narrow left column for metadata (role, dates, technologies in monospace), wide right column for narrative.
- **Lexicon structure:** Each section begins with a large "headword" (e.g., "Brokk", "Kamusi", "Woven") followed by numbered or bulleted "senses." This is not a resume timeline; it is a reference work.
- **Whitespace as punctuation:** Generous vertical whitespace between entries. The page breathes like a well-typeset book.
- **Overlaps:** Syntax tree branches may overlap section boundaries. Marginalia may bleed into the main column at deliberate breakpoints.
- **No cards:** Projects are not presented in uniform cards. Each entry has unique internal structure based on its content — some are dense (Brokk technical details), some are expansive (Kamusi narrative).

### 7. Motion Language

- **Velocity:** Fast, precise fades and slides. Nothing floaty. Elements arrive like typeset blocks being locked into place.
- **Inertia:** Smooth scrolling with subtle momentum. Feels like turning pages, not gliding.
- **Kinetic typography:** Headlines may "parse" — letters separate slightly on load, then lock together. Or words reveal character-by-character on scroll, like a terminal rendering text.
- **Scroll choreography:** Syntax tree branches grow downward as the user scrolls. Project entries fade in from the left margin, as if being indexed.
- **Parallax:** Subtle. Background paper texture shifts at a different rate than content. Syntax tree illustrations have gentle depth.
- **Reveals:** Technical metadata (technologies, dates) appears in monospace first, followed by narrative serif text — mimicking how a linguist identifies parts of speech before reading meaning.
- **No bounce, no elastic overshoot, no whimsy.** Motion is functional and precise.

### 8. Signature Interaction

**The Proofreading Hover:**

When hovering over any project description, the text does not simply change color. Instead:

1. A **margin annotation** appears to the left — like a proofreader's mark — containing a single evaluative phrase in amber (e.g., "Strong architectural thinking," "Novel application of LSP," "Cross-disciplinary impact").
2. Simultaneously, the underlying text gains a **subtle syntax-highlight glow** — nouns in terracotta, verbs in indigo, adjectives in amber — revealing that every description has been "parsed" for quality.
3. The cursor becomes a **caret symbol** (˙) rather than a pointer, reinforcing the editorial metaphor.

This interaction makes Michael's evaluation expertise tangible. The visitor experiences the site as something that has been reviewed, scored, and refined.

### 9. Signature Visual Motif

**The Living Syntax Tree:**

Recurring throughout the site:
- As a **section divider:** A Tree-sitter-style parse tree rendered in thin lines, growing from a single root node into branches.
- As a **background texture:** Faint, low-opacity parse trees behind hero text or project headers.
- As a **hover state:** On skill or technology tags, hovering expands the tag into a miniature tree showing related technologies.
- As a **loading state:** Site assets "parse" into view — elements appear node-by-node, trunk-first, then branches.

The motif is not decorative; it references his actual work with LSP and Tree-sitter while also metaphorically representing how his career branches from a single root (psychology + linguistics).

### 10. Portfolio Storytelling

Projects are not listed chronologically. They are organized into **lexical categories**:

- **"Built"** — Brokk, Recordbay, Uchat, Adalabs (construction, tooling, shipping)
- **"Evaluated"** — Woven (judgment, quality, assessment)
- **"Translated"** — Kamusi Project (language, NLP, cross-cultural systems)

Within each category, entries are ordered by **semantic weight** (most representative first), not date. The visitor reads Michael's career as a **thesaurus of capability** rather than a timeline of employment.

Each project page/section contains:
1. **Headword** (project name)
2. **Pronunciation / Origin** (client, dates, location)
3. **Definitions** (2–3 numbered senses describing what the project was and what it demonstrated)
4. **Etymology** (how it connects to previous work)
5. **Usage Notes** (technologies, constraints, evaluation criteria)
6. **Cross-references** (links to related projects)

### 11. Mobile Interpretation

- **Lexicon collapses to single column.** The two-column editorial layout becomes a single narrative stream with metadata embedded as inline labels (e.g., "React · TypeScript · 2021" in monospace before the paragraph).
- **Syntax trees simplify.** Complex tree illustrations become linear, indented outlines that fit narrow screens.
- **Proofreading hover becomes tap-to-reveal.** On touch devices, tapping a project reveals the margin annotation and syntax highlight for 3 seconds before fading.
- **Typography scales.** Headlines remain large but stack vertically. Monospace metadata becomes pill-shaped tags.
- **Navigation.** A fixed bottom bar with four lexicon-style tabs: Built · Evaluated · Translated · Contact. Feels like browsing a dictionary app.

## DIRECTION 2: THE REMOTE OBSERVATORY

### 1. Concept

Michael is a **precision instrument** — a psychology-trained evaluator and AI engineer based in Nairobi, observing and measuring technical quality for global clients. The site is designed as a **research observatory or meteorological station**: it measures, records, and reports on his own work with the same rigor he applies to evaluating others'.

The metaphor is **scientific instrumentation**: gauges, dials, coordinate systems, and measurement annotations. Every element of the site is "calibrated." The visitor is not just viewing a portfolio; they are reading an **instrument panel** that displays Michael's capabilities as measurable phenomena.

### 2. Emotional Response (First 10 Seconds)

The visitor should feel: **"This person operates at a level of precision I should not ignore."**

Cool, controlled, instrumented. There is a sense of **calibration** — everything is exactly where it should be. The visitor senses that Michael does not guess; he measures. This appeals to engineering leaders, founders, and clients who value reliability over charisma.

### 3. Visual Vocabulary

- **Instruments and gauges:** Circular progress indicators, linear scales, coordinate readouts, measurement tick marks.
- **Blueprint / technical drawing:** Thin blue or warm-gray lines on off-white, annotation callouts, dimension markers.
- **Station identification:** The site feels like a labeled research station — "Station: Nairobi," "Operator: Michael Gikaru," "Disciplines: AI Engineering, Evaluation, Linguistics."
- **Data visualization:** Skill and capability displays use radar charts, constellation maps, or node graphs rather than bars or percentages.
- **Material honesty:** Aluminum, glass, paper — materials associated with measurement and documentation.
- **Nairobi presence:** Subtle cartographic elements — Nairobi coordinates, East African relief contours, or a quiet horizon line.

### 4. Typography

- **Display / Headlines:** A geometric sans with technical precision — *Space Grotesk*, *DM Sans*, or *Geist*. Used at large sizes with tight tracking for instrument labels.
- **Body / Narrative:** A neutral, highly readable sans — *Inter*, *IBM Plex Sans*, or *Geist*. Clinical but warm.
- **Data / Technical / Metadata:** A tabular monospace — *JetBrains Mono*, *IBM Plex Mono*, or *Space Mono*. Used for coordinates, measurements, version numbers, and technical specs.
- **Relationship:** The sans-serif family provides a unified "instrument face." Monospace is reserved for readouts. The typography feels like a **well-designed scientific instrument** — every character has purpose.

### 5. Color Strategy

- **Primary background:** Cool off-white (#F8FAFC) or very light warm gray (#F5F5F0). Feels like a lab notebook or instrument faceplate.
- **Primary text:** Dark slate (#0F172A). High contrast for readability.
- **Accent 1 — Instrument light:** Electric amber (#F59E0B) or warm orange. Used for active states, gauges, and highlights. Like an LED on calibrated equipment.
- **Accent 2 — Signal green:** Muted emerald (#059669) for "operational" status, completed projects, active metrics.
- **Accent 3 — Alert red:** Terracotta red (#DC2626) used sparingly for callouts or evaluation "flags."
- **Neutral:** Cool grays for secondary text, borders, and inactive states.

**Why this palette belongs to Michael:** It reflects his evaluation background (gauges, signals, status indicators) and his remote precision (instrument faces, calibration). The warm amber connects to Nairobi sunlight and the amber of Woven's quality marks. It is not a "tech startup" palette; it is a **laboratory palette**.

### 6. Layout System

- **Coordinate grid:** A visible but subtle grid overlay — like graph paper or a technical drawing. Elements align to grid intersections.
- **Station layout:** The hero section reads like a station ID card: name, coordinates, operator status, disciplines.
- **Panel system:** Projects are displayed in "instrument panels" — rectangular regions with labeled headers, readout values, and status indicators.
- **Asymmetry through calibration:** Elements are not centered arbitrarily. They are positioned by "measurement" — a project image sits at 40% from the left, a gauge at 75%, creating an asymmetrical but intentional balance.
- **Annotations:** Thin lines with arrow callouts point from data to explanations, like a technical manual.

### 7. Motion Language

- **Velocity:** Moderate, mechanical. Animations have the feel of a physical instrument settling — slight deceleration at the end.
- **Inertia:** Scroll-driven parallax with a sense of weight. Elements move as if mounted on slides or rails.
- **Reveals:** Sections slide in from off-screen like drawers opening in an equipment rack. Technical metadata "prints" into view line by line.
- **Hover states:** Gauges shift, needles move, coordinates update. Every hover produces a measurable change.
- **No bounce, no playfulness.** Motion is functional, engineered, repeatable.

### 8. Signature Interaction

**The Calibration Hover:**

On any project or skill element, hovering triggers a **"calibration sequence"**:

1. A thin circular gauge appears around the element, filling from 0% to a specific value over 800ms with a mechanical easing (ease-out with slight overshoot).
2. A **readout** appears nearby displaying a precise measurement — not a fake metric, but a qualitative calibration: "Evaluation Consistency: High," "Cross-Disciplinary Depth: Broad," "Remote Reliability: 5+ Years."
3. A coordinate or timestamp appears in monospace, as if the instrument just took a reading.

This makes Michael's evaluation expertise tactile. The visitor feels they are interacting with a calibrated system, not a static webpage.

### 9. Signature Visual Motif

**The Coordinate Crosshair:**

- A subtle **crosshair (+) or reticle** appears in the background of every section, centered or offset.
- Used as a section divider: a horizontal line with a centered crosshair mark.
- Used as a loading indicator: a crosshair that expands into the full layout.
- Used as a hover state on interactive elements: the cursor area shows a crosshair target.
- The motif represents **precision, targeting, and measurement** — core to evaluation and remote work.

### 10. Portfolio Storytelling

Projects are organized by **measurement category**:

- **"Instrumentation"** — Tools built (Brokk, Recordbay, Uchat)
- **"Calibration"** — Quality systems (Woven)
- **"Translation"** — Language work (Kamusi)

Each project displays:
- **Status indicator** (Active, Archived, Ongoing)
- **Coordinates** (client location, Michael's location at the time)
- **Readouts** (key capabilities demonstrated, measured qualitatively)
- **Signal strength** — a visual indicator of project impact or complexity, not a fake metric

The narrative is: **"Here is what I have built, measured, and translated."**

### 11. Mobile Interpretation

- **Instrument panels stack vertically.** The panel system becomes an accordion or stacked cards with labeled headers.
- **Grid simplifies.** The coordinate grid becomes a single vertical timeline with measurement tick marks.
- **Gauges remain.** Circular gauges work well on mobile — they are compact and readable.
- **Calibration hover becomes tap.** Tapping a project triggers the calibration sequence, which remains visible until dismissed.
- **Typography scales down but retains precision.** The monospace readouts become slightly larger for mobile readability.

## DIRECTION 3: THE DUAL REGISTER

### 1. Concept

Michael is a **bilingual, bifocal thinker**: Builder and Evaluator, English and Swahili, Code and Language, Psychology and Engineering. This direction makes that duality **structural** rather than metaphorical. The site literally runs on two registers simultaneously — a "Builder Register" and an "Evaluator Register" — that the visitor can toggle, blend, or experience in parallel.

The site is a **translation layer** between these registers. Every piece of content exists in both modes. The visitor is invited to switch perspectives, just as Michael switches between building systems and judging them.

### 2. Emotional Response (First 10 Seconds)

The visitor should feel: **"This person thinks in dimensions I haven't considered."**

Intellectual, slightly disorienting, compelling. There is a sense of **depth** — the surface is not the whole story. The visitor realizes they are encountering someone who holds contradictory roles simultaneously and synthesizes them.

### 3. Visual Vocabulary

- **Split-screen asymmetry:** The viewport is divided into two registers — left (Builder) and right (Evaluator) — that may have different backgrounds, typography, or even color temperatures.
- **Register indicators:** A persistent toggle or split bar shows which register is active or dominant. Labels: "BUILD" / "EVALUATE" or "EN" / "SW" or "CODE" / "LANGUAGE."
- **Translation layers:** Hovering over English text reveals a faint Swahili translation or linguistic annotation in the margin. Hovering over code reveals a natural-language explanation.
- **Concrete and paper:** The Builder side may feel slightly more industrial/concrete; the Evaluator side more editorial/paper. The boundary between them is visible but permeable.
- **Marginalia:** The Evaluator side is denser with annotations, scores, and feedback marks. The Builder side is cleaner, more expansive.

### 4. Typography

- **Builder Register:** A modern, geometric sans or slab serif — *Space Grotesk*, *Archivo*, or *DM Serif Display*. Feels constructed, architectural, forward-looking.
- **Evaluator Register:** A classic editorial serif — *Source Serif Pro*, *Lora*, or *Newsreader*. Feels reviewed, refined, authoritative.
- **Code / Translation layer:** A clean monospace — *JetBrains Mono* or *SF Mono*. Used for Swahili glosses, code snippets, and evaluation rubrics.
- **Relationship:** The two typefaces are in constant dialogue. A project name in the Builder register might be followed by an evaluative footnote in the Evaluator register. The visitor reads both simultaneously.

### 5. Color Strategy

- **Builder Register:** Warm concrete gray (#E5E5E5) with terracotta accents (#C2410C) and deep indigo (#1E1B4B). Feels like construction, architecture, making.
- **Evaluator Register:** Warm paper (#F5F1E8) with amber highlights (#D97706) and deep green (#14532D). Feels like review, grading, editorial markup.
- **Translation layer:** Neutral white or light gray, with monospace text in a balanced mid-tone.
- **Boundary:** A visible but thin dividing line — not a harsh black bar, but a warm gray rule with occasional cross-stitch or dashes.

**Why this palette belongs to Michael:** The warm/cool split maps to his actual mental states — the warm energy of building and the cool distance of evaluating. Terracotta connects to Kenyan earth and Kamusi. Amber connects to Woven's quality marks. Green connects to growth and language.

### 6. Layout System

- **Dual-column with permeable boundary:** Left and right columns share a vertical gutter. At certain breakpoints, elements from one side "cross over" to the other.
- **Asymmetric weighting:** The Builder side may have 55% width, the Evaluator side 45%, or vice versa depending on the section. The dominant register gets more space.
- **Overlapping annotations:** On the Evaluator side, marginalia may physically overlap the Builder content, creating a sense that evaluation is constantly happening over the work.
- **No uniform cards:** Projects span both registers — a project "lives" in the Builder side but its evaluation "lives" in the Evaluator side.

### 7. Motion Language

- **Velocity:** Variable. Builder animations are solid and grounded. Evaluator animations are lighter, more floating (like paper).
- **Parallax:** The two registers may scroll at slightly different rates, creating a subtle lenticular or binocular effect.
- **Reveals:** Builder content rises from below. Evaluator content fades in from the left margin, like marginalia being written.
- **Cross-over transitions:** When toggling registers, content transforms rather than swaps — code blocks reflow into natural language, Swahili glosses slide in from the side.
- **Kinetic typography:** On load, the site name or headline may assemble from two halves — Builder letters and Evaluator letters — meeting in the middle.

### 8. Signature Interaction

**The Register Toggle / Lens:**

A persistent control (bottom-center or side-mounted) allows the visitor to:
1. **Lock to Builder** — see only the constructed work, clean and unannotated.
2. **Lock to Evaluator** — see only the evaluation layer: feedback, scoring, reviews, annotations.
3. **Blend** — see both registers overlaid at 50% opacity, revealing how evaluation and building coexist.

Additionally, hovering over any text triggers a **micro-toggle**:
- Hovering over a project description briefly highlights the Builder register.
- Hovering over a technology tag briefly highlights the Evaluator register, showing how that technology was assessed or applied.

This makes Michael's dual identity interactive. The visitor literally switches between his builder mind and his evaluator mind.

### 9. Signature Visual Motif

**The Translation Gloss:**

Throughout the site, select words or phrases have a **gloss** — a smaller, monospace annotation in the margin or below the text:
- English terms may have Swahili glosses (e.g., "evaluation → tathmini")
- Technical terms may have linguistic glosses (e.g., "Tree-sitter → parser: a grammar for code")
- Code terms may have plain-English glosses (e.g., "LSP → language server protocol: code intelligence")

This motif appears in three forms:
1. **Inline:** Small monospace text immediately following a term.
2. **Marginal:** A note in the gutter, connected by a thin line.
3. **Hover reveal:** Appears only on interaction, keeping the surface clean.

The gloss motif represents Michael's **habit of translation** — between languages, between code and meaning, between building and judging.

### 10. Portfolio Storytelling

Projects are presented as **parallel texts**:

- **Builder column:** "What I made" — features, technologies, outcomes, screenshots/code samples.
- **Evaluator column:** "How I judged it" — challenges assessed, quality criteria applied, feedback given, lessons learned.

For example:
- **Brokk** → Builder: "AI-native IDE with LSP and Tree-sitter." Evaluator: "Evaluated prompt reliability across agent workflows; identified gaps in tool-calling context."
- **Kamusi** → Builder: "ETL pipelines for multilingual datasets." Evaluator: "Assessed linguistic data integrity across language families; flagged translation drift."
- **Woven** → Builder: "N/A — this is pure evaluation." Evaluator: "Calibrated scoring rubrics across four engineering disciplines; maintained 99%+ consistency."

The narrative: **"I build, and I also know how to judge building."**

### 11. Mobile Interpretation

- **Registers stack vertically.** The dual-column becomes a vertical toggle: swipe or tap to switch between Builder and Evaluator views of the same project.
- **Gloss becomes accordion.** Tap a term to reveal its gloss inline, expanding the text block.
- **Boundary softens.** The dividing line becomes a labeled tab bar: "BUILT" / "EVALUATED" / "BOTH."
- **Parallax reduces.** The lenticular effect is simplified to a single scroll direction.
- **Typography scales.** Both registers remain legible, but the toggling mechanism is optimized for thumb reach.

## ANTI-GENERIC TEST RESULTS

### Direction 1: The Syntax of Judgment

**"Could this website belong to 10,000 other developers?"**

**No.** 
- The dictionary/lexicon structure is extremely rare in developer portfolios.
- The integration of Swahili script and linguistic notation is specific to Michael's identity.
- The "proofreading hover" interaction is unique.
- The syntax tree motif references his actual technical work (LSP, Tree-sitter), not a generic "code" aesthetic.
- The editorial paper-and-ink palette avoids all common tech-portfolio color schemes.

**Risk:** Requires strong typography and editorial design execution. If typography is weak, the concept collapses.

### Direction 2: The Remote Observatory

**"Could this website belong to 10,000 other developers?"**

**Borderline — with modification.**
- The "scientific instrument" aesthetic is used by many "precision-focused" portfolios.
- The gauge and coordinate motifs, while not universal, are established visual languages for "technical rigor."
- **However:** The Nairobi station identity, the Woven evaluation calibration, and the specific mapping of palette to Michael's domains make it distinguishable.

**Risk:** Could drift toward a generic "engineering precision" trope if not anchored to Michael's specific story.

### Direction 3: The Dual Register

**"Could this website belong to 10,000 other developers?"**

**No — but with caution.**
- The split-screen duality is used in some award-winning portfolios, but rarely with this specific conceptual framing.
- The Builder/Evaluator distinction is unique to Michael's career.
- The Swahili/English gloss layer is highly specific.
- **However:** Split-screen sites are increasingly common. The concept must be executed with extreme refinement to avoid feeling like a 2024 CSS-Tricks tutorial.

**Risk:** Execution-dependent. A sloppy split-screen feels like a gimmick; a refined one feels profound.

## FINAL DECISION

### Primary Direction: THE SYNTAX OF JUDGMENT

**Why this direction best represents Michael:**

1. **It is structurally true.** Michael literally works with syntax (LSP, Tree-sitter), language (Swahili, Kamusi, NLP), and judgment (Woven evaluation). The portfolio's structure mirrors his actual professional activities, not just his personality.

2. **It is visually distinctive.** The dictionary/lexicon format, paper-and-ink palette, and syntax tree motifs create a brand that could not be swapped to another developer. A React developer, a DevOps engineer, or a product manager could not use this direction credibly.

3. **It honors all domains equally.** Psychology becomes editorial rigor. Linguistics becomes typography and notation. Code becomes syntax trees and parse structures. Evaluation becomes proofreading marks. No domain is subordinated.

4. **It scales from intimate to expansive.** The concept works for a single-page resume site and for a multi-project interactive experience. It allows Michael to add new projects as new "dictionary entries" without restructuring the site.

5. **It avoids generic AI-portfolio tropes.** No gradients, no glassmorphism, no floating cards, no terminal windows. The AI dimension is expressed through structure (syntax, language, grammar) rather than visual cliché.

6. **It has a clear editorial voice.** The site does not need to shout "I am an AI engineer." It demonstrates it through the precision of its own design. A site that parses its own content is the strongest possible proof of Michael's parsing capabilities.

### Backup Direction: THE DUAL REGISTER

**Why this is the backup:**

1. **It captures the Builder/Evaluator tension** — the most unusual aspect of Michael's career.
2. **It is highly interactive and memorable** — the register toggle and gloss layer create a "talked about" factor.
3. **It works well if Direction 1 feels too conservative** — it is more experimental and risks more, which could be appropriate if Michael wants to signal avant-garde creativity.

**Condition for using backup:** If Michael's brand positioning leans toward "experimental creative technologist" rather than "precise editorial engineer," Direction 3 becomes the primary.

## CREATIVE NORTH STAR

> The portfolio is a **precision instrument for reading Michael Gikaru's professional identity**.
>
> It must feel like opening a well-worn field notebook belonging to a psychologist-linguist-engineer who has spent five years judging technical work and building language-aware systems.
>
> **Design Constitution:**
>
> 1. **Editorial authority over tech hype.** Typography is the primary visual device. Color is secondary. Animation is functional, not decorative.
>
> 2. **Every element must pass the "syntax test."** If an element cannot be mapped to Michael's actual work — LSP, Tree-sitter, evaluation, Swahili linguistics, psychology — it does not belong.
>
> 3. **Paper, ink, and concrete.** The palette is warm and material. No neon, no gradients, no glass. The texture of the site should feel like paper, a whiteboard, or a concrete floor — something real.
>
> 4. **Dual register awareness.** The site must communicate both the Builder and the Evaluator, the Code and the Language, without choosing one over the other. They coexist.
>
> 5. **Precision is the aesthetic.** Generous whitespace, exact alignment, measured motion. The site should feel calibrated.
>
> 6. **Swahili is structural, not decorative.** Any integration of Swahili script or linguistic notation must serve the information architecture, not just add visual interest.
>
> 7. **No generic developer portfolio tropes.** If it looks like a template, reject it. If it could be another person's portfolio with a different name, reject it.
>
> 8. **Mobile is a reflow, not a shrink.** The concept must survive on a 320px screen through intelligent recomposition, not hidden content or scaled-down desktop layouts.
>
> 9. **Interaction reveals judgment.** The signature interaction (proofreading hover, calibration gauge, or register toggle) should make the visitor *feel* Michael's evaluation expertise, not just read about it.
>
> 10. **The site itself is the proof.** A portfolio for an AI engineer and evaluator must demonstrate engineering rigor and evaluative quality in its own design. If the site is sloppy, inconsistent, or generic, it contradicts its subject.

## NEXT STEPS (NON-CODE)

1. **Confirm name/subject alignment.** Verify whether the portfolio is for Michael Gikaru or Boniface Mwangi before any visual exploration proceeds.
2. **Typography procurement.** Identify and test display serif, body serif/sans, and monospace pairings that meet the direction's requirements.
3. **Color token definition.** Translate the palette strategy into CSS custom properties with accessibility checks.
4. **Motion specification.** Define exact easing curves, durations, and scroll-trigger thresholds for the chosen motion language.
5. **Content architecture.** Map Michael's actual projects and experience into the chosen structural system (lexicon, observatory panels, or dual-register entries).
6. **Interaction prototyping.** Low-fidelity prototype of the signature interaction (proofreading hover, calibration gauge, or register toggle) to validate concept before full implementation.
